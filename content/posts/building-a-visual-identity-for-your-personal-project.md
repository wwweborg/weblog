Starting a personal project is exciting. You have an idea, perhaps a name, and a general feeling of what you want it to become.

Then comes the visual part.

You open a design application, experiment with a few fonts, choose some colors, and eventually create a logo. It looks good, so you move on.

But a few days later, you need a social media graphic, a website header, or a presentation slide. Suddenly, you're making visual decisions all over again.

The problem might not be the individual designs. It might be that they don't belong to the same visual world.

That's where a visual identity becomes useful.

## Start with a personality, not a logo

A visual identity is more than a symbol or a collection of attractive colors. It's the visual language that helps people recognize and understand a project.

Before opening a design application, think about its personality.

Is the project serious or playful? Is it technical, artistic, experimental, or educational? Should it feel traditional or contemporary?

There isn't a universal answer. A personal photography portfolio and an independent programming blog don't necessarily need to communicate the same things.

Try describing your project using three adjectives.

For example:

- Minimal.
- Technical.
- Approachable.

These words can become a starting point for your visual decisions.

They aren't strict rules, but they give you something to evaluate your choices against.

## Color is a system, not a collection of favorites

Choosing colors is often one of the most enjoyable parts of the process. It's also easy to get carried away.

You find a beautiful blue, then a warm orange, then another shade that looks interesting. Before long, you have a palette with no clear purpose.

Instead, assign roles to your colors.

A simple palette might include:

{{< table >}}
| Role | Example | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | `#252A34` | Main brand color |
| Secondary | `#526D82` | Supporting elements |
| Accent | `#F2A65A` | Highlights and emphasis |
| Background | `#F5F5F2` | Main surface |
| Text | `#202124` | Readable content |
{{< /table >}}

The specific colors are only an example. What matters is that each one has a reason to exist.

You should also consider contrast, especially when the palette will be used in digital interfaces.

A visually attractive combination isn't automatically an accessible one.

## Typography gives the identity a voice

If color establishes an atmosphere, typography helps determine how that atmosphere communicates.

A geometric sans-serif might feel modern and technical. A serif typeface can introduce a more editorial character. A monospaced font can suggest a connection with programming or technical documentation.

But choosing a typeface isn't enough. You also need to decide how it will be used.

For instance, you could establish a small typographic system:

- One typeface for headings.
- Another for body text.
- A monospaced family for technical details.

You don't need five or six different fonts to create visual variety.

Consistency often makes a stronger impression than constant experimentation.

## The logo is only one part of the identity

It's tempting to treat the logo as the most important element of the entire project.

And while it certainly matters, a visual identity should work even when the logo isn't visible.

Think about a website without its header logo. Could someone still recognize its visual personality through its typography, colors, spacing, illustrations, and overall composition?

That's a useful way to evaluate the strength of your design system.

A good identity should be flexible enough to work across different formats:

- Website and blog.
- Social media graphics.
- Presentation slides.
- Documents and reports.
- Profile images and favicons.

The logo needs to adapt to these contexts, but it shouldn't be responsible for carrying the entire identity by itself.

## Create a few rules you can actually follow

You don't need a hundred-page brand guidelines document for a personal project.

A small reference sheet can be enough.

Document your decisions somewhere accessible:

- Your main and supporting colors.
- Your chosen typefaces and their roles.
- Logo variations and minimum sizes.
- Common spacing values.
- Illustration or photography style.
- A few examples of layouts that represent the identity.

This becomes particularly useful when the project grows or when you return to it after several months.

{{< callout type="info" title="A practical tip" >}}
Keep a small visual reference file inside your project repository. It can contain color values, typography choices, logo exports, and links to design references. That way, your decisions don't disappear between projects or design sessions.
{{< /callout >}}

## Let the identity evolve

One thing worth remembering is that a visual identity doesn't have to be perfect from the beginning.

Personal projects change. Their content grows, their audiences evolve, and sometimes their original visual direction no longer makes sense.

That's normal.

The goal isn't to create something that can never change. It's to establish a coherent starting point that makes future decisions easier.

A visual identity should provide direction without becoming a limitation.

And perhaps that's the most useful way to think about it: you're not just designing a logo or choosing a palette. You're creating a visual language that gives your project room to grow.
